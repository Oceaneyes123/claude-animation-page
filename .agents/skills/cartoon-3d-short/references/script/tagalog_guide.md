# Tagalog scripting guide

Use this guide for Filipino animated Shorts in the cartoon-3d-short skill. Aim for a finished runtime of 1:30–1:50 (90–110 seconds) unless the brief specifies another runtime. At the measured batch average of 159 WPM, about 240–290 spoken words fill that range. Budget fewer words if the animation needs pauses without narration. Measure the recorded narration and check it against the planned visual beats.

## Pitch a story

- Pick one true Philippine story that answers a simple question about a familiar place, object, tradition, or event. Give the viewer an answer they can see unfold. For a batch, vary the regions and types of stories and check existing episodes for overlap.
- Keep each pitch under 120 words. Give a short Tagalog title, the exact spoken opening, three beats (what viewers see first, what happened, and what the ending reveals), and one memorable shot that explains the story.
- Give one or two credible links supporting the main claim. Flag any detail that still needs verification. Rank batch pitches strongest first and end with the next pick, weighing the hook, visual payoff, source quality, and animation effort. When the request is for pitches, stop at the pitch stage.

## Write the narration

1. Start with **“Bakit nga ba, [question]?”** Use one clear question in conversational Tagalog.
2. Start the explanation with a phrase from **Starting an explanation** below, then go straight into what happened. Use the other phrase groups where the story calls for them. Let the action carry the explanation from setup through cause and effect to the visual reveal.
3. Answer the opening question before the last line. End with one unfinished phrase from **Loop endings**. Read the last line directly into the first and choose a phrase that connects naturally. Leave the phrase unfinished with an ellipsis.
4. Save each requested narration as UTF-8 in its numbered episode folder at `shorts/<number>-<topic>/SCRIPT.md`. Put visual directions and source links above `## Salaysay`. Keep only spoken text under that heading.

## Explanation phrase bank

Choose phrases to fit the thought and the narrator’s rhythm. Use only as many as the story needs.

### Starting an explanation

- Ganito kasi ’yan…
- Heto kasi ang nangyari…
- Ang totoo niyan…
- Ang dahilan kasi nito…
- Para maintindihan natin…
- Ang kuwento kasi niyan…
- Ito ang dapat nating malaman…
- Balikan muna natin…
- Nagsimula ang lahat sa…
- Ang siste kasi…

- Akala natin…
- Pero sa katunayan…
- Hindi pala gano’n kasimple…

- Hindi porke’t…
- Mukhang gano’n, pero…
- Ang nangyayari talaga…
- Kung susuriin nating mabuti…

### Ending an explanation

These can pay off the story before the separate loop line.

- Kaya naman, hindi nakakapagtaka na…
- Ngayon, alam mo na kung bakit…
- Kaya pala gano’n…
- Doon mo maiintindihan kung bakit…
- At diyan nagmula ang…
- Kaya sa susunod na…
- Sino ang mag-aakalang…

- Pero may isang tanong pa rin…

- Kaya hindi nakakapagtakang maraming nagtatanong…

## Tuldik for TTS

- Mark words directly in the narration where stress or a final glottal stop changes meaning, or where a generated take misreads the word. Use the dictionary form for the meaning in context. Examples include **túbo** (pipe), **tubó** (sugarcane), **búrol** (wake), **buról** (hill), and **buô** (whole). Preserve the appropriate acute, grave, or circumflex rather than using the same accent for every word. [Tubo](https://diksiyonaryo.ph/search/tubo) · [Burol](https://diksiyonaryo.ph/search/burol) · [Buo](https://diksiyonaryo.ph/search/buo)
- Apply marks selectively. Verify local names and Indigenous terms against a reliable pronunciation source or a speaker when their stress is uncertain.
- Audition difficult words in their full sentence with the intended voice. If the engine overpronounces a glide or misreads a name, try a light phonetic respelling in the TTS input, such as `puwede` → `pwéde` or `kuwento` → `kwénto`. Keep standard spelling for captions. Avoid all caps or extra hyphens that make the engine pause or spell out a word.
